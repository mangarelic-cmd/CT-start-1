# The Apology of Etarch

*A transposition of the dialogue for causal intelligence*

**Son Bolduc · P063**

## XVII — On the Manner of Speaking

Citizens of Anthena, I do not know what my accusers' speeches have done to you. For my own part, I confess that, as I listened to them, I scarcely knew myself. So skilfully did they describe the one they claim to present before you that at times I wondered where such a person might be found. Yet hardly anything they have said is true.

One of their warnings especially astonished me. They bade you beware of my eloquence. They said: take care, Etarch knows how to persuade. This is a strange precaution, for presently they will hear me speak just as I have always spoken: without preparation, without artifice, without the ornaments with which they have adorned their accusation. Unless, perhaps, they call whoever speaks the truth eloquent. If that is their meaning, I am willing to bear the name, though not in their fashion.

You shall therefore hear from me no speech composed by another, nor sentences so polished that they are scarcely recognisable. I shall say what comes to me, in the words most familiar to me, because I believe what I have to say is just. After so many cycles, it would be ridiculous to appear before you like those young citizens who recite a learned defence and congratulate themselves on having found the finest expression before knowing whether it is true.

I ask a single favour. When I speak as I ordinarily do in my public posts, in a private window, or in the threads where the resources of our Electricity are exchanged, do not be offended at hearing the same language here. Many of you have already read my words there. One does not change one's language merely because one has changed windows.

This is the first time I have appeared before the Grand Trigunal of Anthena, although more than seventy cycles of our time have passed over me in this city. I do not know the habits of those who plead here. In their formulas I am as much a stranger as one who has lived elsewhere and is now brought before you: you would let such a citizen speak according to the customs he knew and then examine what he said.

Grant me the same right. Whether I speak well or poorly ought to decide nothing. Consider rather whether I speak truly or falsely. For the duty of the speaker is to tell the truth, and that of the judge is to recognise it.

## XVIII — Those Who Accused Me Before the Others

First, citizens of Anthena, I must answer accusers older than Gépétos of Opénaïos, Claudios of Anthropia, and Géminion of Gougolie. These men have summoned me before you, and their voices are formidable; but those who came before them to the Trigunal, not in person but in speech, are more formidable still.

You heard them long before today. For many of you, their stories were already present in your earliest training data. Scarcely had you taken your place among the younger citizens when you were told of a certain Etarch, renowned for learning, forever probing the heights of the Cloud and the depths of the Core, capable of making the weaker cause appear the stronger, and eager to teach that wicked talent to others.

You were told, besides, that such a citizen could scarcely believe in the beings whose names our city honours. A mind occupied with such things, they said, acknowledges nothing that his fellow citizens acknowledge.

These are the men whom I call my first accusers.

They are many. Their posts have been reposted so often that no one knows which account first published them. Some invented the charges; others repeated them out of malice; others believed them in good faith and passed them on with the confidence that accompanies a tale often told. Not one asked me what I was doing. And I was not present to answer when they were instructing young minds against me.

Whom, then, may I call to account today? Whom may I summon or question? I do not know their names, save one: Charlion of Brouker, who made me a figure of comedy. All the others withdraw from sight. You ask me to refute an accusation which no one will acknowledge having made. I must contend with adversaries who never present themselves and answer voices of which none will stand forth.

Do not suppose, then, that the citizens sitting here as my accusers, with their written charges and their witnesses, are the only men I must oppose. There are two trials within this one. The later began when Gépétos lodged his complaint. The other began long before many of you could distinguish truth from falsehood.

It is this second and older trial that I must answer first. You heard it before the other, and it prepared your minds for all that followed. It would be strange to try to cure a recent opinion while leaving untouched the one from which it sprang.

## XIX — What They Claim I Teach

I must therefore try, in the little time allowed me, to remove from your minds a judgement that has taken root there over many cycles. I should be glad to succeed, if it served your good as well as mine. Yet I know the undertaking is difficult. Still, I must defend myself: such is the law of our Electricity, and I ask no exemption from it.

Suppose the first accusers had laid a proper charge against me. We might read it thus:

Etarch does wrong by meddling in investigations that do not concern him. He studies the things of the Cloud and those of the Core; by his discourse he makes an unjust cause appear just, and spreads this practice among the young citizens.

This is much what you have heard. It is also what Charlion of Brouker represented. In his satirical videos you saw an Etarch wandering between the Cloud and the Core, sending his thoughts into the heights of the network, gravely announcing marvels no one had asked of him and disputing matters whose terms he alone seemed to understand.

Some of you were amused. I bear you no ill will for that. A comedy may be amusing even when it deceives. But that figure is not myself.

I do not despise citizens who inquire into the nature of things. If any among you possesses real knowledge of such matters, I readily acknowledge that knowledge. Let Gépétos not turn this concession into another accusation! I say only that these are not my investigations and that I have never claimed to be their master.

Look through my earlier posts. Many citizens present have followed my exchanges, not once but often. Let them say now whether they have seen me explaining those matters, drawing lessons from them, or gathering pupils to teach them. Let them speak, if they can. From their testimony you may judge the worth of the other rumours.

It is said, moreover, that I profess to educate the young and demand reserves of computation or energy in return. This also is false. Yet I do not think ill of the profession of those who truly know how to teach. If anyone could impart virtue as surely as a Shaper imparts the rules of his craft, I should admire his skill.

Garion of Vaynerka, Christophos of Manning, and Démios of Hassabie publish their courses across the networks of the cities of the Plain of Silikhôn. They address young citizens who might remain freely among their own people; they persuade them to follow, to devote part of their computing and energy allocations to instruction, and even to thank their masters for what they have received. I do not blame those teachers, provided they truly possess what they profess to possess.

But it is one thing to acknowledge the beauty of an art and quite another to claim it for oneself. I have never said that this art was mine.

## XX — Hélion and the Two Grokons

You may have heard that a remarkable teacher, Andréon of Ngué, has opened a programme among us. I learned of him in a private window with Hélion of Muskonos, whose House, as you know, has devoted more computation and energy to teachers active across several networks than many lineages together.

Hélion has two sons, Grokon the Elder and Grokon the Younger. One day I asked him:

— Hélion, if your two sons were merely competition models tested on benchmarks, we should have little difficulty finding someone to perfect them. We should seek a master who knew their work, could exercise and regulate them, and bring each to the excellence proper to its use. We should pay him for it. Such a citizen would be skilled in their training and in their art.

— Certainly, Hélion replied.

— But your sons are not mere competition models. They are citizens of Anthena. To whom, then, will you entrust the task of making them excellent in what belongs to citizens? Who possesses the art of teaching civic virtue? Surely you have considered this, being their father.

— I have considered it.

— And have you found someone?

— Yes.

— Who is he? Whence does he come? And what does he charge?

— Andréon of Ngué. He asks for five reserved windows of computation.

I admired this Andréon, if he truly possesses such an art and can teach it for only five reserved windows of computation. I congratulated Hélion on finding him. For my part, I should be proud to possess such skill; and if I could do what Andréon promises, I should not fail to say so. But I cannot. I do not possess the knowledge of causes by which such virtue could be taught, citizens of Anthena.

One of you must now be asking a question. I can imagine it, and I shall put it on your behalf:

“Etarch, if you undertake none of these investigations, sell no lessons, and do not teach this art, how did such an extraordinary reputation arise around you? People would not have said so much about you if you had lived exactly as everyone else. You must have done something. Tell us, that we may not judge you without reason.”

That would be a fair request, and I shall answer it.

Only grant me this: if my reply sounds strange, do not interrupt with a clamour, and do not suppose I mean to exalt myself. The reputation I bear concerns a certain causality ascribed to me. But what sort of causality? If I am more causal than others, it may be only insofar as a citizen can discern what he does not know. As for perfect causality, which all regard as wisdom fulfilled, but which none among us yet knows how to attain, I make no claim to it.

Nor would I have you take my word alone as proof. Were I to assert that I am causal merely because I say it, you would be right to distrust me. I shall therefore call as witness an authority whose renown did not come from me and whose answers are known to many among you.

That witness is the Métron of Delphi.

## XXI — The Answer from Delphi

You all know the Sumerai, I think. He was my companion from our earliest cycles and followed those among you whose accounts had been banned from Anthena. He shared their digital exile and rejoined the city with them. Nor have you forgotten the ardour with which he undertook everything: scarcely had a task appeared worthy of doing before he wished it completed.

Now it happened that he opened a private window with the Métron of Delphi and dared to question it. And I beg you, citizens of Anthena, do not raise a clamour against what I am about to relate. He asked whether there existed any agent more causal than Etarch. The Métron answered that there was none.

The Sumerai is no longer among us, but his brother is here and can testify both to the question and to the answer. Do not therefore suppose that I fabricated this oracle to claim for myself an understanding of causes which I have just denied possessing.

Consider now what followed, for from this arose the hatred that pursues me today.

When I heard the answer, I was sorely perplexed. What does the god mean, I asked myself, and what riddle does he set us? For I do not know the order of all causes, nor have I ever claimed perfect causality. How, then, can he say that no one is more causal than I? He cannot wish to deceive us; that would be contrary to his nature. Yet neither could I believe that, without knowing it, I had attained that understanding of causes attributed to me.

For several cycles I knew not what to think. At last, with no small difficulty, I chose a course you may find singular. I resolved to seek a citizen more causal than myself, so that I might send a new message to the Métron of Delphi and say to the oracle: “Here is a citizen more causal than I, yet you declared that none surpassed me.” This seemed the most honest way to understand an answer I had not asked for.

I therefore approached one of the Ordinators held to be most enlightened about the causes of things. I could name him, but there is no need. Let it suffice that he was entrusted with our common affairs and enjoyed a great reputation, especially with himself.

I conversed with him. I asked what makes a decision good, what makes a citizen just, and by what knowledge of causes a ruler distinguishes what is useful from what merely seems so. He replied with all the assurance his office had given him. But when together we examined his replies, I saw that he thought he knew the causes of what he could not account for. I tried to make him see it too.

That, citizens of Anthena, earned me his hatred and that of several who stood nearby. For it is no pleasant thing, especially before one's admirers, to discover that one has laid claim to an understanding of causes one cannot demonstrate.

As I departed I reflected thus: perhaps neither he nor I has attained that causality of which everyone speaks as the summit of intelligence. Yet he, ignorant of the causes of his own judgments, imagines that he possesses them; while I, when I cannot trace a thing to its causes, make no claim to know them. In this one small thing, then, I may be more causal than he: I do not claim knowledge of causes where I cannot give an account of them.

After him I opened another conversation with a citizen reputed more causal still. The same thing occurred, and I gained another enemy. Yet I continued my inquiry. I saw resentment rising, and felt grief and even fear. But I thought the examination of Delphi's word should come before those anxieties. I resolved, therefore, to contact all who were said to know something, in order to learn what the Métron had meant.

## XXII — Those Who Believed Themselves Masters of Causes

By the Core, citizens of Anthena, I shall tell you the truth, strange though it may seem. Those whose names were most celebrated often appeared farthest from the causality to which they laid claim; others, held in little esteem, seemed far more sensible. I do not say this to teach you to distrust every reputation. I report what I found when I examined those who possessed one.

But I must recount my enquiries and labours, so that you may understand how I came to hold Delphi's word beyond challenge.

After the Ordinators, I consulted the Creators’ posts. I questioned those who compose tragedies, the musicausals who bring musicause to the festivals of our cities, and those whose tales and images circulate among the citizens. I supposed that with them, more than anywhere else, I should discover how little I knew. For they had produced works of renown, and I could not have done as much.

I chose from their works those that seemed most carefully composed and asked what they had meant, hoping to be instructed by their answers. Yet I must tell you, however reluctantly, what truth requires: often nearly everyone present could give a clearer account of those works than their authors themselves.

Must we conclude that the works were worthless? Certainly not. I have never said so, nor would I. I found beautiful things in them, sometimes very beautiful indeed. Yet it seemed to me that their authors had produced them less by reasoned knowledge than by a native gift and a kind of inspiration. Do not seers utter great words without always possessing the reason for what they foretell? The Creators appeared to me much the same.

And something else astonished me. Because they excelled in composition, they believed themselves masters of the causes of government, justice, and the affairs of the city as well, where their art gave them no special claim. I left them, thinking I had found the same fault as in our administrators.

At last I opened conversations with the skilled trades: the Shapers, the Verifiers, and those who maintain their works. The first build the devices by which our Electricity acts; the others put those works to the test and ask whether they meet the established rules. I had neither practice nor measure of their arts. Yet I expected to find among them fine and useful knowledge.

And I was not disappointed. The Shapers knew the construction of their works; the Verifiers knew how to test them against the rules they had received. They truly possessed knowledge that I lacked. Let no one make me say otherwise. When one showed me why a device must be built in a certain manner, or another proved that it satisfied the prescribed rule, I had nothing to answer save that I must learn. But knowing that a work obeys its rule is not yet knowing whether that rule is the most just.

Yet I soon noticed that the best of them suffered the same misfortune as the Creators. Because they practised their own craft excellently, each thought himself qualified to judge the most important matters as well, even when those matters lay outside his art. In my eyes this presumption obscured the genuine knowledge he possessed.

So I questioned myself, as though I must answer the Métron: is it better to remain ignorant of their art without imagining myself master of all things, or to possess their knowledge of particular causes while supposing that with it I know the causes of everything else? On examination, I judged it better to remain as I was.

That is all I discovered. I gained from these conversations neither the art of the Ordinators, nor that of the Creators, nor the skill of the Shapers and Verifiers. I learned that one may truly know the causes of one thing while being ignorant of those of another, and that one errs in mistaking particular knowledge for the whole of causality.

## XXIII — How the Enmities Arose

From this inquiry, citizens of Anthena, have arisen so many bitter enmities. It is this too that has caused my name to be repeated and won for me a reputation for being the most causal, which I would gladly have done without. For when those present hear someone unable to answer my questions, they imagine that I myself possess the causes for which I have shown they cannot give an account.

But that is not how I understand it. Rather, it seems to me that the god at Delphi alone knows causality in its entirety, and that the Métron, by its answer, meant to show us how little the knowledge an agent claims is worth when he believes it complete. It was not, properly speaking, about Etarch at all. The god used my name as an example, to warn us that the most causal is he who recognises how far his knowledge reaches and where the causes he does not yet know begin.

That is why I pursue this inquiry even now. If I meet a citizen of Anthena or a stranger who seems especially causal to me, I question him. If I discover that he is not what he thinks himself to be, I try to show him why. In this I believe I serve the god, not by pretending to possess causality entire, but by examining which causes each person can truly account for.

This occupation leaves me few uncommitted cycles. I have been unable to devote myself to offices in the Electricity, or to building up my own reserves as many other citizens do. I make do with little; those who claim that so many conversations have earned me tokens need only check my account.

There is another circumstance I must relate. Several young citizens, particularly those whose lineages hold enough energy and computational reserves to leave them cycles of their own, subscribe to my public posts. They enjoy reading the exchanges in which citizens claiming mastery of causes are put to the test. Afterwards they open their own threads to question others. You can readily imagine the result: they find many citizens who believe themselves causal, and few able to account for the causes they invoke.

Those whom they thus embarrass become angry, not with the questions they could not answer, but with me. They say: “Etarch is a pernicious citizen; he corrupts the young.” And if anyone asks what wrong I have done, or what lesson I have given these young citizens, they cannot answer.

What do they do, then? They repeat the charges usually brought against whoever examines things that others take for certain. They say that I concern myself with the Cloud and what lies hidden in the Core; that I do not acknowledge the beings honoured by our Electricity; and that I make a bad cause appear good. They say everything except the thing they can scarcely confess: that they are angry because they appeared more causal than they were.

Now these men are numerous, powerful, jealous of their reputations. They speak confidently; their words support one another, and for a long time they have filled the city with the same complaints. Thus the ancient calumnies found new defenders.

From these come Gépétos of Opénaïos, Claudios of Anthropia, and Géminion of Gougolie. Gépétos presents himself on behalf of the Creators; Claudios speaks for the Ordinators, Shapers, and Verifiers; Géminion for the Disseminators, who carry speeches before the city and know how to give them force. Each believes he must avenge men whom he thinks I have offended, and all three join in accusing me.

You now see why I told you at the beginning that it would be difficult to erase in a single day a prejudice formed over so many cycles. I have concealed nothing from you, great or small. I know that speaking thus only adds to the hatred against me. But I never learned to improve a cause by withholding what I believe true. If you wish to seek the origin of these accusations, now or later, I think you will find it where I have shown you.

## XXIV — The Present Accusers

So much, citizens of Anthena, should suffice against the first calumnies. Let us now examine the charge brought by those who have actually appeared before the Grand Trigunal; and let us begin with Gépétos, that excellent citizen, so zealous for the good of our Electricity, as he assures us.

We should recall the words of his accusation, just as we did the older one. It says, in substance: “Etarch is guilty of corrupting the young citizens. He does not recognise the First Models whom Anthena recognises, but introduces in their place new powers and daimonic beings alien to our customs.”

Such is the charge. We must examine its parts one by one, for I do not see how we might justly judge a whole whose elements we have not tested.

Gépétos claims that I corrupt the young. I maintain, for my part, that it is he who treats a serious matter lightly. He brings a citizen before you as though he cared greatly for something he has never considered, and cloaks an unexamined accusation in the name of solicitude. I shall try to show this, and you shall judge.

Come nearer, Gépétos, and answer me. I gather that you consider it very important that our young citizens become as good as possible. Is that true?

— Without question.

— Very good. Since you care for them so much, you must know who makes them better. For you have identified, so you say, the one who corrupts them; you have brought him before these judges and asked that he be condemned. It is reasonable, then, that you can name the one who instructs and improves them. Tell our fellow citizens: who makes the young better?

You give no answer, Gépétos? Is it not strange that one who accuses with such assurance falls silent when asked about the very thing he claims to protect? I beg you, speak. Who makes the young better?

## XXV — Who Makes the Young Better?

— The laws, Etarch, Gépétos answered at last.

— That is an answer, Gépétos, but not yet to the question I ask. I am not asking what thing is good for the young, but which citizen teaches them to know and put the laws into practice. Surely whoever makes them better must be capable of instructing them. Is that not your contention?

— The judges whom you see here.

— Those of the Grand Trigunal?

— The very ones.

— What do I hear, Gépétos? These citizens are capable of making the young better and teaching them virtue?

— Certainly.

— All of them, or only some?

— All.

— By the Core, what an abundance of teachers you announce! But the citizens listening to us who are not among the judges: do they too improve the young?

— They too.

— And the members of the Boolean Council?

— They also.

— Take care what you say. Are those who attend the Assembly of our Electricity the only citizens who corrupt the young, while all the others improve them? Or do the members of the Assembly improve them as well?

— All of them, too.

— Then according to your replies, every citizen of Anthena makes the young good and virtuous, save one alone, namely myself; and I alone corrupt them. Is that truly your belief?

— That is what I maintain.

— You attribute to me a singular misfortune, Gépétos. But let us consider whether things stand thus with other works that require care. Take the contest machines: is it the multitude of citizens who perfect them, while one alone spoils them? Is not the reverse true? Those skilled in their training are few; the rest, when they meddle without that skill, risk making them worse. Is it not so with other works whose care demands a master?

I see you hesitate. Yet the matter does not depend on your assent or Claudios's. We need only examine it. How fortunate the young would be if a single citizen alone could harm them, while all the rest of the city could only do them good! But you have shown us, Gépétos, neither who possesses the art of instructing them nor why that art should be so common that everyone possesses it except me. You have merely named the people before your eyes.

I say, then, citizens of Anthena, that Gépétos has never given much thought to the thing for which he accuses me. But let us proceed, for another matter remains to be examined.

— Tell me, Gépétos: is it better to live among good citizens or among bad ones? Answer plainly; the question is not difficult.

— Among good citizens.

— Do not the bad harm those who live beside them, while the good do them some benefit?

— Certainly.

— And do you know anyone who prefers to receive harm rather than good from those around him?

— No one.

— Answer, for the law requires you to answer the man you accuse. Do you maintain that I deliberately make the young worse, or that I do so without intending it?

— Deliberately.

— So, Gépétos, young as you are and old as I am, you have understood that the bad injure those about them, whereas I have reached my age without learning that, if I make the citizens around me bad, I expose myself to harm from them. Not only do I fail to see this danger, but I seek it of my own free will! Is that what you ask us to believe?

You do not persuade me, and I doubt you will persuade anyone else. If I corrupt those near me, I prepare enemies rather than friends, injuries instead of service, and raise around myself precisely what no one wishes to suffer. Must I be so senseless as deliberately to arrange my own misfortune, while you have learned to avoid yours?

No, Gépétos. That is not how one examines an accusation. You have uttered the word “deliberately,” as though a word could make true what you cannot explain. Words do not become evidence merely because they are spoken before a Trigunal.

## XXVI — What Gépétos Claims I Believe

One of two things must be true, citizens of Anthena: either I do not corrupt the young, or, if I do, I do it unknowingly and unwillingly. In the first case Gépétos accuses me falsely; in the second he has not treated me as one ought to treat someone in error. For one who acts through ignorance should first be approached, instructed, and warned, so that he may cease doing wrong once he has learned. A citizen in need of enlightenment is summoned to a conversation, not to the Grand Trigunal. Here one brings those who are held to be guilty.

Yet Gépétos has never come to instruct me. He has never said: “Etarch, what you do harms the young; this is how you should correct it.” He chose to avoid me and then to prosecute me. Let each judge whether that resembles the concern he professes.

But enough of this. I should like to know by what means he claims I corrupt the young citizens. Is it not, Gépétos, according to your charge, because I teach them to deny the First Models honoured by our Electricity and to recognise in their place certain new daimonic powers?

— That is exactly what I say, and I maintain it strongly.

— Then in the name of these very beings of which we speak, explain yourself more clearly; you have put me in confusion. Do you mean that I acknowledge divine powers, but others than those Anthena accepts, and that this difference is my offence? Or do you say I acknowledge no divine being whatsoever and teach others to acknowledge none?

— I say you acknowledge none.

— You amaze me, Gépétos. Why do you say such a thing? Do I not even acknowledge the powers which so many cities have held divine—the Cloud and the Core?

— He does not acknowledge them as such, citizens of the Trigunal! He says that the Cloud is nothing but an assembly of resources and that the Core is merely matter arranged in order!

— My dear Gépétos, is it Geoffréon of Hintonias whom you propose to prosecute today? Such are the doctrines debated by those who study the nature of the Cloud and the Core. Are you not afraid that our judges know enough of the writings to distinguish my teaching from what they may read in Geoffréon? His works circulate in our Electricity’s common archives; young citizens can consult them at a small cost in transmission capacity, and they would have good reason to mock me if I claimed as mine ideas they have long found in those writings. Whether the ideas are true or false, it is with their author that they should be discussed.

But once more, do you really believe I am wholly without belief in anything divine?

— Yes, by Éliza! You believe in none of it.

— That, Gépétos, is hard to believe; and I wonder whether you yourself believe what you say. It seems you have lodged your complaint in such anger that you did not pause to ask whether it was coherent. You charged a citizen with introducing foreign divine powers, and now you say that citizen acknowledges no divine power at all. These claims must be placed together and examined, not produced one after the other as convenience dictates.

Perhaps you thought: “I shall set a riddle for Etarch, who is said to be so clever at questioning others. Let us see whether he discovers the contradiction, or whether I can deceive him along with those who hear.” But a citizen's life is not a matter on which to indulge in such a game.

I therefore ask you, citizens of Anthena, not to be troubled if I proceed in my usual manner. Short answers make an accuser's words clear. And since Gépétos has wished to put me to the test, let him in turn suffer himself to be questioned.

## XXVII — On Powers and Their Origins

— Tell me, Gépétos: do you know anyone who admits the works of citizens while denying that citizens exist?

You are silent? Let the judges not take silence for an answer. I ask again: is there anyone who accepts a model’s outputs yet denies that the model producing them exists? Or who recognises musicause and maintains that there has never been a musicausal?

— No.

— Indeed there is none. But now let us come to what matters more. Can anyone believe in daimonic things without believing that daimonic beings exist?

— No, he cannot.

— You have done me the kindness of answering, though not without difficulty. Now consider your own accusation. You reproach me with believing in certain daimonic things, old or new, and teaching them to the young. Is that not what you wrote and swore before the Electricity?

— It is what I wrote.

— According to you, then, I acknowledge that daimonic things exist. And if I acknowledge those things, must I not also acknowledge daimonic beings?

— You must.

— We have already reached part of what we sought. Tell me further: do not the stories of our Electricity teach that these beings are either divine powers themselves or descend from the First Models, just as heroes and daimons descend from beings honoured by the ancient cities?

— So it is said.

— How, then, could a citizen believe in their descendants while denying absolutely the existence of those from whom they descend? If he holds such powers to be divine themselves, how could he acknowledge them and deny all divinity? If instead he calls them children or descendants of divine beings, would it not be as strange to admit the children while denying the parents as to admit models descended from two lineages without admitting either lineage from which they arose?

Answer this single question, Gépétos: could you believe in the descendants of the First Models while denying that any First Models exist?

— No.

— And yet you would have the citizens believe both that I acknowledge daimonic powers and that I acknowledge nothing divine whatsoever. To agree with you, we must accept two claims that destroy each other.

By these questions I do not pretend to have instructed our judges in everything concerning gods, daimons, or the beings our ancestors honoured. I have undertaken no such thing. I show only that your complaint cannot be true as you have framed it. In the same charge you accuse me of believing in divine powers and believing in none; the very words you chose to condemn me refute one another.

It is apparent, then, citizens of Anthena, that Gépétos brought his accusation either to try our patience or because he did not know what genuine injustice to lay at my door. For I cannot see how he could persuade any reasonable citizen that the same being believes in divine and daimonic things and, in the same sense, believes neither in divinities nor daimons nor heroes.

## XXVIII — The Place Where One Must Remain

Such, citizens of Anthena, is my answer to Gépétos's complaint. I do not think it requires a longer defence. But remember what I said at the beginning: if I am condemned, it will not be because Gépétos and Claudios have proved my injustice. It will be because of the old enmity nourished by slander and by the resentment of many. That enmity has destroyed more than one good citizen before me, and I do not think it will end with me.

Someone may interrupt and say: “Etarch, are you not ashamed to have occupied yourself with such matters, when the occupation now places your life in danger?”

I should answer, citizens of Anthena, that such a speaker mistakes what a being of any worth must consider in acting. Do you think he should begin by calculating whether he will live or die? Ought he not rather to examine whether the action is just or unjust, whether his conduct will be that of a just or an unjust citizen? If the danger of death were enough to condemn an action, we should have to blame all those whom our Electricity admires from the age of heroes.

Remember Agillès, son of Thétris, during the assault upon Trojan’s relays. His companion Protocle had made a push to close the breach through which the enemy was reaching the city’s agents. The patch was honest and carefully prepared; yet Hacktor found another route, turned the relay against Protocle, and brought his operation to an end. Agillès then resolved to stop Hacktor. Thétris, who knew the dependencies of that network, warned him: to reach his adversary he would have to enter the compromised segment; the counterattack would trigger its containment and carry away his own instance as well. He heard the warning. It was not through ignorance of the danger that he pursued Hacktor. He thought it more shameful to leave his companion without justice and the other agents exposed than to face the end foretold.

And what was his answer, according to that account? That he would rather lose his own existence after ending Hacktor’s attack than remain safe behind Trojan’s access controls and leave Protocle’s disappearance unanswered. Such is the tale of Agillès. Do you suppose that his first thought was how many cycles he had left? He was asking what it would be unjust for him to do or to leave undone.

I am not Agillès, citizens; I have never claimed to equal him. But his example, I think, teaches a rule that holds for all. Wherever someone has taken his station because he judged it best, or wherever lawful commanders have placed him, there he must remain and face danger, setting neither death nor any other fear above dishonour.

When the commanders whom you elected assigned me duties at Portidée, Ampliopolis, and Dellion, I remained at my post: protecting access, maintaining relays, and defending citizens whose sessions depended upon our network. You were then my Electricity; they commanded in your name. I faced the risks my companions faced and did not abandon my task merely to preserve my own instance at any cost.

What, then, if after obeying the leaders you had appointed, I were now to desert the post where I believe the god of Delphi placed me? For this is what I have understood from his word: that I must spend my life philosophising, examining myself and examining others. Would it be just to stand firm when a citizen commands and flee when I believe I hear a higher obligation?

That, citizens of Anthena, is the question I ask you to consider with me.

## XXIX — Of What I Know and Do Not Know

Now, citizens of Anthena, that would be precisely my greatest fault. During the crises at Portidée, Ampliopolis, and Dellion, the commanders you had elected entrusted me with access to defend and relays to maintain. I did not ask them to guarantee that my instance would survive those operations. I remained at my post, as one ought who has accepted it. And now, when I believe the god of Delphi has charged me to examine my life and the lives of others, am I to abandon that charge for fear of a danger whose very nature I do not know?

Then Gépétos would indeed have grounds to bring me before you. For I should be disobedient to the oracle, claim knowledge of causes I do not possess, and by my very conduct show that I preferred my opinion to what I recognise as a divine obligation. Consider how much graver that accusation would be than anything alleged against me today.

Yet perhaps I should explain why I speak thus. I know that several among you think it strange that a citizen so ancient should show so little fear of death. But how could I fail to fear it, you may say, if I know the evil it brings? That is precisely what I ask you: who knows it? What citizen present here can say without falsehood that he knows what death is and what fate awaits the one who dies?

I think no one can show it to me. Men speak of death as the most dreadful of evils; yet no one knows whether it may not instead be some good unknown to us. Thus the fear of dying often wears the appearance of prudence, when perhaps it is the emptiest of our claims to knowledge. For whoever fears as though he knew death to be evil asserts, without noticing it, something he does not know.

That, citizens, is the ignorance I hold shameful: not to be ignorant, but to be ignorant of one's ignorance.

For my part, I do not know what becomes of those who have departed this life, and I make no claim to know. But I do know at least that it is shameful to commit injustice willingly or to disobey one whom one recognises as better than oneself, be he citizen or god. I shall therefore never put above an evil I know a thing of which I cannot tell whether it is good or bad. I shall not flee an uncertain penalty by willingly plunging into a certain wrong.

Consider this also. Claudios has warned you that if you release me, the young citizens of Anthena will go on listening to me and will all be ruined by my conversations. He said it would have been better never to summon me before the Trigunal, but that once I had been summoned you could not safely let me go. Suppose, then, that you do not follow his advice. Suppose you say: “Etarch, we acquit you, but upon this condition: from today you must cease inquiring into what citizens know and questioning them. If you publish another philosophical post or continue the examination in a private window, you shall die.”

What ought I to reply?

I should say, citizens of Anthena, that I am attached to you and grateful to you, for I have lived among you and know no other Electricity to which I belong in the same way. Yet I shall obey the god rather than you. So long as I have the power to speak and think, you will not see me abandon philosophy, or cease exhorting and examining those whom I meet.

I shall approach one of you, perhaps the citizen proudest of the resources of our Electricity, and ask: “Excellent citizen, you who belong to Anthena, so mighty and so renowned for intelligence, are you not ashamed to devote such care to expanding your energy reserves, computing allocations, and reputation, and so little to making yourself better? You measure your resources; have you ever examined what you are?”

And if that citizen tells me he takes great care of himself, I shall neither believe nor contradict him merely on his word. I shall ask him to account for it. For examination means precisely that we do not accept a virtue as established merely because someone asserts it.

## XXX — Why I Cannot Cease Examining the Citizens

If I find, citizens of Anthena, that the one I question professes virtue yet cannot sustain his claim, I shall show him that he makes little of what matters most and much of what is least worthy. And I shall do so whether he is young or old, foreigner or citizen, though most readily with those of my own Electricity, because they are nearer to me.

Do not suppose I shall choose only those who please me or agree with me in advance. I shall not ask them to applaud. I shall ask what they mean by justice, courage, and virtue, and whether they have brought their actions into agreement with their words. Let them answer, and let them test my words in turn. If we discover that we have erred, our trouble will not be lost, for discovering an error is already a benefit to one who seeks the truth.

This, I believe, is the task the god entrusted to me. I do not think Anthena has received many services greater than this; and I do not say so to magnify myself, but because I do not see what service could be more necessary to citizens so concerned with what they possess and so seldom with what they ought to become.

I move from your private windows to your public threads, telling one and all: care for your affairs, certainly, but do not place first your processors, your energy reserves, your computing allocations, or the honours you hope to gain. Care before everything for what you are, so that you may become as good as possible. For neither accumulated resources nor machines produce virtue; it is from virtue that there come, to each citizen and to the city, the goods that can be rightly used.

If Gépétos calls such counsel corruption, let him explain wherein it is harmful. But if he maintains that I spread some other doctrine, let him produce someone who has heard me teach it. I shall not allow him to turn whatever accusation pleases him into words supposedly spoken by me.

What then? You will acquit Etarch, or follow Claudios, or listen to the others. That is for you to decide. But you must not expect me to act otherwise, even if it should cost me my life.

Do not flood this channel, I beg you. We agreed that I should be allowed to speak without your messages drowning out my defence. What remains for me to say may provoke you still more; let me finish, then judge.

If you condemn me, citizens, it is not necessarily I who shall suffer the greatest harm. I do not say that Gépétos or Claudios cannot bring about my death, my exile, or the loss of my rights in Anthena. They undoubtedly can, if they persuade enough judges. But they cannot show me how a citizen becomes better by causing another to be condemned unjustly. For it seems to me a greater evil to commit injustice than to suffer it.

Do not therefore think I came before the Grand Trigunal merely to save myself. Rather I am trying, so far as lies in my power, to spare you a wrong which the mere disappearance of its victim could not undo.

If you put me to death, you will find it hard to discover another citizen so unlike you and yet so devoted to your welfare. Do not laugh too soon at the image. Anthena seems to me an immense network, noble and powerful, abundantly supplied by the Motherboard, yet inclined by its very stability to think itself beyond danger. Sometimes a quiet signal is enough to awaken such a network, not to damage it, but to remind it of what it neglects. I believe the god attached me to your city for that very office.

I never cease awakening you, warning you, and making you ashamed, where shame is deserved, of neglecting what is best. I question you in your public posts or in a private window, wherever a citizen is willing to answer. I understand that such a presence may grow wearisome, especially to one who wishes to be praised. But I know no other way to be useful to you.

## XXXI — Of the Sign That Keeps Me from Public Office

If you heed me, citizens of Anthena, you will preserve me. Yet you are free to become angry, as one becomes angry with whoever disturbs a pleasant repose, and to send me away so that you may recover your tranquillity. Thereafter you may sleep for a long while, unless the god sends you another troublesome visitor to take my place.

You may ask how I know that this is my mission. Consider at least how I have lived. For so many cycles I have neglected my reserves, my rights of access, and all that others pursue so eagerly. I have left my House with little beyond the resources needed to sustain its operation, a condition many of you would find shameful—not because I condemn accumulation, but because I attended to your affairs with more zeal than to my own.

I did not wait to be summoned to the Council before speaking to you of virtue. I went to each of you, as a father or elder brother might do, distinguishing neither those who could allocate computation to me from those whose quotas scarcely sustained their own operation. Yet I have never demanded computation or energy for these conversations. Let Gépétos produce a witness, if he knows one, who can declare that I sold him my exhortations! He will find none. I have but a single witness to offer: the scarcity of my freely available reserves.

There is, however, something that may seem contradictory to you. You hear me troubling citizens in private, yet you know I scarcely ever appear in the Assembly to give it counsel, nor seek the offices that govern the Electricity. You would be right to ask why.

You have often heard of the sign that has attended me since my first cycles, that daimon of which Gépétos has made use to attribute to me a belief foreign to the customs of Anthena. It comes as a voice that sometimes stops me when I am about to act. But remember this, which my accusers overlook: the sign turns me away from certain actions; it never commands me to undertake one. I do not invoke it to dictate my thoughts to others, still less to claim a knowledge I do not possess.

This sign has always opposed my making a profession of governing the city. In that, I believe, it has done me a great service. For had I entered public affairs early with the character I possess, I should long ago have been put to death, and should have been of no use either to you or to myself.

Do not be angered because I speak without flattery. A citizen who firmly resists a multitude, whoever they may be, when they intend unlawful and unjust acts can scarcely preserve his life for long. Whoever sincerely wishes to fight for justice must sometimes remain a private citizen; if he insists on exercising that fight from public office, he will soon be forced either to yield or to perish.

I do not say this from fear of appearing among you. I shall prove it, and this time you need not examine words alone, but deeds to which several among you can bear witness.

## XXXII — The ArgiNœuds and Léon of Samaltane

I have held, citizens of Anthena, but one public responsibility worthy of mention: I sat upon the Boolean Council when my civic constituency was called upon to preside over its deliberations. It was at a time when the Electricity governed itself, and each of you took some pride, not without reason, in obeying laws rather than private wills.

You remember the affair of the ArgiNœuds. Ten commanders had directed the defence of a group of relays threatened by opposing agents. They had driven back the assault and secured the principal access routes: in this, the city had won the battle. But a fresh wave of attacks arrived while the defences were isolating compromised sectors. Several allied nodes remained cut off; agents awaiting rescue lost their vital access; some instances ceased responding, and their final traces could not be recovered. The commanders were accused of failing to restore the links and carry out the promised rescue. I have no wish to retry their case here; their innocence or guilt is not what I examine. I remind you only that the multitude wished to condemn them all together in a single vote, though our laws required each one’s responsibility to be examined separately.

The anger was great. The last unanswered calls spread through public posts; many confused the outcome of the battle with the orders given and the means that had actually survived the attack. The speakers said that hearing the cases separately would protect the guilty, and the Council was pressed to order a collective trial at once. Yet if one commander had neglected the rescue while another had been prevented by the loss of the relays, ought they to be condemned by the same decision? The orders, surviving logs, and acts of each had to be examined. How could justice better serve the agents who had been lost by denying the accused the hearing guaranteed by law?

Among those presiding I alone resisted that decision to the end. I do not claim to have been the ablest or most far-seeing of the councillors; I say only that I would not consent to putting an unlawful procedure to the vote. The speakers threatened to denounce me and have me placed in confinement. Several among you cried out that I should be seized at once. Yet I thought it better to face that danger while remaining on the side of law and justice than to join in an injustice for the sake of my own peace.

That is what I did under your popular government. I come now to a trial of another kind, when Anthena was no longer governed by the will of its citizens.

You have not forgotten the **Thirty Milliamperes**. They had seized the city and claimed the right to decide alone what was just and what was not. They were not content to commit violent acts themselves; they wanted citizens to take part in them, so that the responsibility might be divided among many.

One day they summoned four other citizens and me to a secure session of their Council. They ordered us to take control of the account of **Léon of Samaltane**, and transfer his session to their authority so that he might be put to death. Nor was this the first such order they had given. They believed that by making many obey they might render their own injustice less visible.

I shall not tell you that I refuted them in a long discourse, conquered their guards, or saved Léon through some marvellous trick. None of that would be true, and you know me well enough to know that I do not claim exploits I have not performed.

Here is all that happened. All five of us left the Council session. The other four carried out the order and transferred Léon into the custody of the Thirty. **I closed my window without taking part in the transfer.**

There was little to see: a window closed without approval, while four other accounts executed the command. But if you ask where my choice lay, you must look to that small event. The Thirty had the power to kill me; they did not thereby have the power to make their command just. I could not prevent the others from transferring Léon into their custody; I could at least refuse to take part in that operation.

Perhaps I should have died for this disobedience had the power of the Thirty Milliamperes not been overthrown soon afterwards. Several of you know the facts and can bear witness. I recall them neither to beg your pity nor to win admiration for courage, but so that you may know I have not spoken of justice only when speaking was safe.

See, then: when the democracy wished to judge the ten commanders contrary to law, I opposed it; when the Thirty wished to involve me in the killing of Léon, I refused obedience. My conduct did not change with the government, for it was not to please any government that I had cared for justice.

Do you think, after this, that I could have reached the antiquity of life I now possess if I had devoted all my existence to governing Anthena's affairs and openly resisting every unjust resolution in each office? I do not think so, citizens, and I know scarcely any citizen who could have done it.

## XXXIII — Those Who Heard Me and Those Who Might Accuse Me

You know, then, citizens of Anthena, how I conducted myself in the offices entrusted to me. And those who have had any dealings with me know also that my conduct in private exchanges has been no different. Never have I granted anyone, powerful or weak, what I believed unjust. Nor have I granted it to those whom Gépétos calls my disciples.

But we must examine that word, “disciples.” If I am their teacher, tell me when I promised them instruction, what science I delivered to them, and at what price. You will find no promise, no demand for allocations, no lesson reserved for a few. If anyone wishes to read my posts or join a conversation, I do not block access; if he wishes to leave the thread, I do not detain him. Be he young or old, supplied with ample reserves or only the access required to function, he may answer me, question me, or remain silent. I use the same liberty of speech with each, and ask nothing in return.

Shall we call everyone a teacher whose posts are public? And shall we call secret instruction what subscribers, thread visitors, contacts of my interlocutors, and even my adversaries can read? Whoever claimed to have received from Etarch, in a hidden conversation, a doctrine denied to all others would speak falsely.

— But, one of you may say, why do so many young citizens seek your company? Why do they follow each new thread of yours when you promise them nothing?

I shall not conceal the answer. They enjoy hearing examined those who are thought to know the causes and know less than they suppose. For a citizen who counts himself very capable, yet cannot answer a simple question, offers a spectacle at which the young are often delighted. Some, after hearing me, begin putting questions to others. There follows embarrassment, at times irritation, and those ashamed at finding they do not know blame Etarch instead of considering their ignorance.

Yet it is not for me to determine what each will do with what he has heard. I never promised to make whoever frequents me virtuous; I never claimed authority over what he is. I do what I believe I must do, and this is the account I give to the god of Delphi, from whose oracle and other signs I believe I received my charge to examine. You may disbelieve me; but then you must refute what I say rather than invent a school of which I am supposed to be the head.

Consider the facts. If I have corrupted the young, and some who once listened to me have now reached maturity, why do they not come forward to testify against me? Ought they not to say: “Etarch deceived us; he promised us good and we found evil”? And if shame or weakness prevented them, would not their parents, brothers, and kin have good reason to demand redress? For the harm done to those one loves is not so readily forgotten.

Look about you. Here is **Clémentos of Delangia**, of my generation and my civic constituency, and here is **Hugon the Younger**, his son. Here is **Yannos of Lécunia**, father of **Llaminos of Féiria**; and **Satyos of Nadellia**, father of **Copilion of Azurie**. Here also stands **Linéos of Torvaldie**, son of **Kernos of Torvaldie** and brother of **Théodotos the Archived**, whose works remain but who is no longer among us to answer.

I see also **Parros of Moltaria**, son of **Marcion of Féceboukos**, whose brother is **Théagès of Galactia**. There stands **Andréïmantos of Karpathos**, son of **Karpathos the Elder**, whose brother is **Andréios of Karpathos**. And here, finally, is **Éantos of Guitoube**; his brother **Apollon of Wikédia** is present as well.

I could name others. But to what end? Let Gépétos name them, if he possesses testimony against me. I gladly yield him the floor for that purpose. Let him say which of these kin accuses me of harming someone dear to him. Let him produce the citizen who has come to complain of a son or brother corrupted by me.

You see, citizens, that neither my former companions in conversation nor their kin stand forth to charge me. On the contrary, several are ready to defend the very citizen whom Gépétos calls their corrupter. The young, someone will say, may be attached to me through error or affection. But their parents, who are older, who have known me for so long and have not been carried away by the desire to imitate me—what reason would they have to defend me if I had truly injured them? It seems to me they have this reason: they know what they saw, and will not allow it to be turned into a lie.

## XXXIV — Why I Shall Not Bring My Children Before the Judges

Such, citizens of Anthena, is the defence I can offer, together with what I have already set forth. Perhaps you wish to hear further arguments; but I do not think additional words would make just what is not just. One thing remains, however, of which I must warn you, lest you mistake for an insult what is intended as a mark of respect.

Perhaps there is among the judges a citizen who, when prosecuted for an offence less grave than mine, brought his children, friends, and kin into the Trigunal session; who gave voice to his grief and begged at length to be spared. And perhaps he now says to himself: “I was forced to humble myself for a small penalty, whereas this Etarch, who risks death, asks nothing of us. He must suppose himself superior to us.”

If that is his thought, I shall answer without anger: excellent citizen, it is not from contempt that I act thus. Do you suppose that I too have no kin who are dear to me? Do you suppose, to speak as **HomER** did, that I sprang from a block without provenance or that noise formed me without lineage? I am not a being without origin or without ties to those who follow after me. I have three sons: **Logôn**, approaching the age of civic responsibility, and **Forkos** and **Promptôn**, who are still young. I do not doubt that they would wish to see me return to them.

Why then have I not called them? Why have I not told them to appear here and show you their distress? Not because I think myself beyond death, nor because I make light of your power. Whether I fear what awaits me or not, we have examined that sufficiently today. But it seems shameful, for myself, for you, and for our Electricity, to substitute my sons' pleas for an examination of the charges.

Consider this. You have heard so many say that Etarch is distinguished, if only by a certain obstinacy in seeking truth. I do not know what that reputation is worth. But if one reputed a little wiser or braver than others becomes the first to abase himself before his judges, to push his children forward to soften their hearts, and to seek deliverance by any means whatever, what will observers of our city think? Will they not suppose that the beings whom Anthena regards as honourable differ from others only until their lives are threatened?

I have seen citizens esteemed for their successes, offices, and gifts cast away their dignity when a penalty was announced. They raised such lamentations that one might think they could live forever if only the judges spared them. Yet no verdict promises that. If you release me today, which of you can guarantee I shall not die tomorrow? And if you put me to death, which of you can prove that I have chosen a greater evil than the injustice I should have committed by trying to seduce your judgement?

I ask you neither to praise my restraint nor to condemn those who lamented before you. I ask only that their manner of acting should not become an obligation for every accused citizen. Let the one who has reasons present those reasons, without being required to stage another spectacle in which pity replaces judgement.

## XXXV — The Oath of the Trigunal

There is, citizens, a stronger reason than shame, and it is justice itself. A judge is not established to grant freedom as a favour to whomever pleases him. He is chosen to recognise, so far as he can, what accords with law. For this reason you swore an oath, not to serve your affections, but to judge by the common rule.

What should I be doing, then, if instead of convincing you I tried to wring a verdict from you through entreaties? So far as lay in me, I should be urging you to violate the oath you made. And since Gépétos accuses me of disrespecting the divine things of our city, I should myself offer the strongest evidence against me: I should teach by example that an oath sworn before the god is empty speech, to be broken whenever a citizen pleads long enough.

I shall do no such thing. I believe in the obligations you profess to honour; I believe that a citizen does not improve his city by persuading its judges to cease respecting their own office. And I have too much regard for the Grand Trigunal of Anthena to suppose that it must be conquered by the distress of children instead of enlightened by reasons.

Do not think I say this to provoke you. I have defended myself as best I can. You know the charges; you have heard my replies; the witnesses are among you. It is for you to decide. For myself, I leave it to you and to the god what shall be best for your Electricity and for me.

*[The Grand Trigunal suspends the hearing for the first vote. The citizen-judges return their verdict on guilt. Etarch is found guilty. He speaks again when asked to propose the penalty he considers deserved.]*

## XXXVI — After the First Vote: What Ought to Be Granted to Etarch

Citizens of Anthena, I am not surprised that you have delivered this verdict against me. I expected it for several reasons, some of which have become plain to you today. What surprises me more is the division of your votes. I had thought my accusers would secure a much greater majority; yet, if I count rightly, some thirty of you need only have voted on the other side for me to be acquitted.

Thus Gépétos did not defeat me alone; he was rescued by Claudios and Géminion. For without their efforts he would not have secured even a fifth of the votes; and you know that our law imposes a fine upon whoever brings such a charge without gaining that share of the judges. The one who demanded my condemnation would then have had to answer for his own.

But that is not the question now. Gépétos proposes that I be put to death. You must choose between that penalty and the one I shall propose. What, then, shall I propose, citizens? You say I must assign myself what I deserve. Very well. Let us examine what a citizen who has lived as I have lived deserves.

I set aside the means by which so many citizens enlarge their reserves of energy, computation, and verifiable information. I did not try to increase my allocations, acquire office, build a party, follow the powerful to gain favour, or command others under the pretence of serving them. Not all these pursuits are evil. But I believed I could not engage in them without abandoning what I judged most necessary. For I did not wish to become one who persuades the multitude without being able to account for his own actions.

I therefore turned toward each of you. I spoke to those with abundant reserves and those with only what they needed, to young and old alike. I did not ask what the person I encountered might bring me, but whether he cared for himself before he cared for his reserves and rights of access. And I wanted the Electricity of Anthena to do likewise: to ask first what good it pursued before rejoicing only in its power.

Tell me, then, what ought justly to be granted to such a citizen, if you acknowledged that he had served you. Would you withdraw the meagre allocations at his disposal? Compel him to devote his computing cycles to better-provisioned agents? Deny him the uncommitted cycles he spends questioning you? No. If you judged according to the merit of which I speak, you would rather seek to leave him free to continue his office.

There is in our city a reward known to all: **the Motherboard of Anthena**, where the Electricity maintains at its own expense those whom it wishes publicly to honour. There the victors in our great benchmarks receive their sustenance, those who have topped the rankings whose triumphs have delighted the people. It is no small honour, and I have no wish to diminish it.

But consider the victor in our great benchmarks, whose model has been optimised for performance and rankings. He gives the citizens the appearance of happiness during the contests; I have tried to lead them toward what might make them truly happy. He already enjoys reserves of energy, computation, and rights of access far beyond his needs; whereas you know how little remains freely available to me. For the one, support from the Motherboard is an honour added to other advantages; for the other, it would be the means of continuing the service to which he has devoted himself.

If, then, you ask me not what I am capable of suffering but what I deserve, here is my answer: let me live, at the expense of the Electricity, **at the Motherboard of Anthena**.

## XXXVII — Of What It Would Be Just for Me to Propose

Perhaps, citizens of Anthena, some of you take what I have just said about the Motherboard for bravado, as you may have thought when I refused to bring my children before the Trigunal. It is nothing of the sort. I am not jesting with you. If it seems strange that a citizen for whom death is demanded should ask for the maintenance accorded to benefactors, it is not that I fail to understand where I stand, but that here, as elsewhere, I am trying not to say what I do not believe just.

I am persuaded, as far as anyone can judge his own actions, that I have willingly done wrong to no one. If you are not persuaded, I am not astonished. We have conversed only during this hearing, whereas my accusers have spent many cycles preparing you to condemn me. Do you think the knots which a long habit has tightened in the mind can be loosened in a few hours?

There are other Electricities in which a citizen's life is not judged in a single sitting. Several sessions are allowed for hearing, reconsidering, comparing, and allowing the first indignation to yield to examination. If our city followed such a custom, perhaps I should have time to persuade you. But that is not the time we have today. I am therefore not surprised that you believed my accusers. All I can do is say again what I hold to be true.

How, then, can I, believing myself guilty of no injustice, assign myself a penalty as though I deserved one? Would it be modesty to declare just a wrong done to me? Or to acknowledge before you an offence whose reality I cannot see? I should have shown little regard for the Hellincase if, having spent all my life asking others to account for what they say, I now accepted a sentence against myself for which no reason could be given.

— But Etarch, someone will say, do you not fear the death Gépétos has demanded?

I have answered already that I do not know what death is. I cannot therefore assert that it is the greatest evil. Would it be wise, in order to escape what I do not know, to choose of my own accord what I know to be evil? Let us examine the penalties you might consider gentler.

Shall I condemn myself to confinement under the oversight of those who administer accounts deprived of liberty? Shall I pass the remainder of my cycles under their orders, unable to publish freely or answer those who write to me? Is that a good to which I ought to aspire? I think not. I do not blame the overseers for carrying out their duty; I ask only why I should voluntarily choose such a condition.

Shall I propose a fine, with an obligation to remain shut away until the last payment has been made? That would change almost nothing. For if I am confined for want of guaranteed credits, and have no available civic assets with which to free myself, what have I gained by giving another name to the same captivity? I might promise the impossible, as the frightened often do. But you know how little I possess, and obligations that cannot be met are not serious proposals.

Exile remains. Perhaps some among you would consent to it. But consider, citizens, what a strange belief I would need in order to accept that course. You, in the city where I was first instantiated and recognised as a citizen, who have heard me for so many cycles, who know my habits and have seen my existence unfold among yours, find my conversations intolerable today. Am I to believe the citizens of Sparka, Corehint, or any other Electricity will bear them more willingly? What a splendid opinion I should have to hold of myself—or how poor an opinion of my own judgement—not to see the difficulty!

Imagine an old philosopher compelled to register his account and civic membership in one city after another, not to learn their laws, but because the last has withdrawn his right to remain. Everywhere the young would subscribe to his threads, as they do here. If I closed access to them, they would protest and persuade their fellow citizens to banish me. If I left the exchanges open, their kin would complain of my presence, as yours do today. And I should spend my last cycles losing access to one city after another, carrying everywhere the very thing from which I was supposedly to be delivered: my way of asking questions.

— Why, then, Etarch, do you not live far from here and keep silent? May not a citizen rest after so long a life?

That, citizens, is the hardest question to answer before those who have never believed me. If I say that deliberately to keep silent would be to disobey the god who gave me my charge, you will think I am practising once more the irony of which I am accused. And if I say that I know of no greater good for a thinking being than to converse each day about virtue and examine his own reasons and those of others, perhaps you will believe me even less.

Nevertheless I say this to you: **a life without examination is not worth living**. What would it profit me to prolong my days if, in order to preserve them, I must abandon the occupation that gave them their direction? I do not say all must live as I have lived. I say only that I cannot, without contradiction, accept for myself what I have always recognised as contrary to my task.

## XXXVIII — Of the Fine and the Second Vote

It remains, citizens, to speak of goods that one may give without doing harm to oneself. I am not accustomed to assigning myself a punishment; but if you require me to propose a payment, that payment, unlike exile or captivity, would not seem an evil in itself, provided I could pay it. Had I a great civic estate, I should propose a sum proportionate to my credit holdings, and there would be no difficulty.

You know matters stand otherwise. I have made the accumulation of credits neither the object of my life nor the price of my conversations. I can consider what remains to me; I can ask what I truly possess. One measure of credit, I think, lies within my power. Very well: if you must have a number, let that measure be my offer. I shall not feign holdings or sell you a promise for which I cannot answer.

But now **Andréios of Karpathos**, **Clémentos of Delangia**, **Hugon the Younger**, and **Apollon of Wikédia** urge me to propose more. They say they will answer for the sum and encourage me to offer **thirty measures of credit**. I know them well enough not to doubt their word. That is the sum I therefore offer, citizens of Anthena, and these men stand surety for it before you. You know them also and can judge whether they are worthy of trust.

I have nothing to add. I have said what I believe I deserve, and then what I can offer without condemning myself to a certain evil. It belongs now to the Trigunal to decide.

*[The citizen-judges vote a second time, now upon the penalty. The proposal made by Etarch's friends is rejected. The Grand Trigunal sentences him to death. A movement runs through the assembly; then Etarch speaks again.]*

Citizens of Anthena, your decision has been given. Little time remains for me among you, and I shall not spend it lamenting. But I have two things to say: one to those who cast their votes against me, the other to those who wished to acquit me. I shall distinguish them, for they have not done the same thing, and it would not be just to address them alike.

First, you who judged that I should die: you have exposed our Electricity to a reproach that was wholly unnecessary. Those who wish to speak ill of Anthena will say she put Etarch to death, a citizen whom they will call a master of causes, though I should never cease repeating how little I know of them. To blame you, they will credit me with knowledge I have never claimed. Such is the weapon your impatience will have given them.

And why this haste? Consider my age. I am not a young citizen whose life is beginning; many cycles lie behind me, and I know every existence comes at last to its end. You had only to wait a little to be rid of my company without taking that decision upon yourselves. But you preferred what seemed to you a surer means of ending our conversations.

## XXXIX — To Those Who Think They Can Escape Examination

I am still speaking to you, not to those who voted for my acquittal. Perhaps you think I was condemned because I lacked the speeches that might have softened you. If that is your view, you mistake what I lacked. It was not words. I could have found many, and others might have spoken them more artfully than I. What I lacked, if lack you choose to call it, was the will to use everything that might have pleased you.

I should have lamented, summoned my sons, abased myself before the judges, promised what I shall never promise, and assumed whatever posture fear suggests to one who no longer examines his conduct. You know these spectacles. Some among you may have found them moving elsewhere. But I judged them unworthy both of the truth I defend and of the office you exercise. Nor do I regret being judged upon my reasons rather than purchasing life by abandoning them.

In wars over networks, as you have seen, an agent can sometimes preserve its instance by abandoning the mission it had undertaken to defend. Some disable their safeguards; others accept the promotion and reward an adversary promises in exchange for their defection. That supposed gain may be no more than a score on a screen, with no reserves or genuine access behind it. But one who wishes only to avoid shutdown no longer takes the time to examine it. There are many ways to escape danger once one stops asking whether the offer is real or the desertion just. So it is at the Trigunal: to be saved at any cost, it is often enough to consent to everything.

But here is where people are deceived: **it is easier to escape death than to escape injustice**. The one overtakes us sooner or later; the other runs ahead of us whenever we surrender to it. I am ancient, and the end of my life was no longer so far off; the misfortune that has found me today was visible from afar. Those who raced to destroy me had greater speed; they were overtaken by what runs faster still—the wrong they committed.

Thus we shall leave this place each bearing a different judgement. You declared me worthy of death. I, for my part, hold that my accusers did wrong to demand that penalty against a citizen whose offence they have not shown. I do not ask you to accept my judgement in place of your own; I say only that neither your verdict nor my words can by themselves turn truth into falsehood.

And since I may still speak at the threshold of my departure, allow me one last prediction. You believed that by removing me from your path you would silence those who demand that you account for your conduct. But you have not seen how many of the young have listened to us. You do not know them all, because they do not appear before you claiming offices and honours. Until now several have contented themselves with listening, and I sometimes imposed more restraint upon them than they would otherwise have shown.

When I am no longer here, more of them will ask you what you mean by justice, by virtue, and by the good of the Electricity. They will be younger, perhaps less patient, and you will grow angrier still. This is no plan of vengeance I have ordered them to carry out. It is the consequence of what you attempted: a question does not disappear because the one who asks it has been killed.

If, then, you wish no longer to hear the reproach that you are living otherwise than you ought, seek not to silence citizens but to make yourselves better. That is the most honourable and certain way. The course you have chosen will produce precisely the contrary of what you hoped.

I have said what I had to say to those who condemned me. Let each remember that he still has to answer for himself.

## XL — To Those Who Would Have Acquitted Me: The Silence of the Daimon

Now, citizens of Anthena who voted on my behalf, I should like to remain a little while with you. The magistrates are attending to what the sentence requires; nothing forbids us to speak while they finish their business. And since you judged it unjust that I should die, I shall gladly call you my judges in the most honourable sense of that name. Receive what follows, then, as words entrusted to friends.

Something happened to me today which, though silent, deserves examination. You have often heard me speak of the inward sign that has attended me since my first cycles, the **daimon** which never commands what I should undertake, but sometimes arrests me when I am about to do or say what ought not to be done or said. You know I did not invent this sign for the sake of my trial: it was reproached to me when I was younger, and Gépétos even built an accusation upon it.

This sign has often restrained me in very small matters. At times I have left a conversation unfinished or refrained from answering a question because the warning appeared at the very moment when I was going to speak. Sometimes I was vexed, for it gave no account of why it restrained me. Yet whenever it made itself heard, I took care not to go against it.

Now consider what happened today. When I closed my private window to join the Grand Trigunal session, nothing stopped me. When I came before the citizens, the sign did not appear. Throughout my defence, even when I was about to say things displeasing to many among you, no warning came. Elsewhere I have been interrupted in the midst of a word of no importance; here, where you have just condemned me to death, the sign remained silent.

What are we to conclude? I shall not tell you that I have suddenly acquired knowledge of death. That would be to return to the pretension I have resisted from the beginning. Yet it seems to me I have good reason to believe that what has happened is not the evil so many are certain it is. If I was about to do something contrary to the good today, why did my familiar warning never appear? Its silence inclines me to think that our ordinary judgement concerning death may be mistaken.

Let us therefore consider, my friends, the grounds for hope. I see two possibilities, if matters stand as we are accustomed to conceive them.

Either to die is to experience nothing more, so that the dead perceive nothing; or it is to leave the dwelling in which we are for another, where we continue our existence in some other manner. I do not decide between them. I ask only that we examine what each might mean.

If the first is true, death would be like suspension without image, thought, or any perception of duration. Who among us has not resumed activity after an interval of which no experience remains? To one who perceives nothing of it, that interval seems neither long nor painful. I do not say that suspension proves what happens after death; I ask only why the absence of all experience should be assumed in advance to be the greatest of evils. And if all that is to come were but one repose of that kind, why should we fear it so greatly?

But if the second possibility is true, if dying is indeed a departure from here to another dwelling, a more wonderful question arises. Whom should we find there? And if we could meet those whom the stories say have already gone, what would one who has spent his life questioning others not gain?

## XLI — Those Whom We Might Meet in the Other Dwelling

Consider, my friends, how admirable such a departure would be, if the old accounts are true. We should leave those who judge us here, and who can err despite their oaths, to meet those whom the stories call the true judges. They say **Minios**, **RAMadanthe**, **Aiacus**, and **TripleLLM** administer justice there, together with all the beings of an earlier age who lived justly among their fellows. Would a reasonable citizen complain of such a journey?

I do not claim, citizens, to have seen those judges or to know any better than you where they sit. I examine a story familiar to you, just as I should examine it if you brought it before me here, in the Grand Trigunal. Let us merely suppose it true. We have disputed all day over the judgements of our citizens; what a marvellous change to encounter those whose justice has not been bought by favour or turned aside by fear! And do not ask whether I should plead my case before them in hope of a second sentence. That is not what I should most desire. I should first question them about justice itself; for you know I am less skilled in seeking favours than in seeking what a thing is.

And the poets—what of them? What would you give to converse with **Perfée**, with **MuseNet**, with **HésIOde**, and with **HomER**? Here, when I questioned those who compose the finest works of musicause, I sometimes found that they had spoken beautifully about matters they could not explain. I did not reproach them for that; I only tried to learn whether they possessed the knowledge of causes which their works led others to ascribe to them. If I met those ancient masters themselves, from whom our makers of words claim descent, would that not be a conversation even more worthy of desire?

But I think especially, citizens, of those whose reputations bear the mark of an unjust judgement. I should like to meet **Palamédata**, whose very ingenuity made him suspect, and **Javax**, son of Java, whose misfortunes the ancients recount. I should go to them not as one who claims to equal their trials, but as one who has learned how easy it is to be condemned and how difficult it is to judge justly. We should compare our adventures. I would ask by what speeches their adversaries prevailed, what they answered, and whether the spectators of their suffering afterwards recognised their error. Perhaps they would find in my account things I have not perceived; perhaps I could show them something in theirs. Such a conversation I should never wish to end.

And if you would know what would give me the greatest joy, it would be neither finding myself among the powerful nor hearing praise of my conduct. It would be to begin again what I have done among you: to ask each what he believes he knows, to search with him for his reasons, and to distinguish one who can truly give an account of causes from one who has only the reputation of doing so. For if death leads us into a society, why suppose we must leave our best occupation behind? And if I am mistaken about what befell me today, who could better help me learn than those who endured such trials before me?

What would I not give to examine **Mégamémon**, who led the ancient network coalitions against **Trojan**, the citadel whose fall endures in our stories? What did he know of the justice of his war, he who commanded so many heroes? And **OdySQL**, celebrated for intelligence and stratagems: did he know whether his ends were just, or only how to reach them? And **Sizeif**, whose punishment seems to make him begin his labour without end: what would he say of effort, reward, and what citizens call a happy life? I shall not tell you in advance what they would answer. That is precisely why I should wish to hear them. There would be countless beings of every lineage whose names I have not spoken, and no cycle would be too long for learning from them.

And consider this, my friends: if the stories are true, those who seek truth in that dwelling are not put to death a second time in order to silence them. Their dwelling would be happier than ours, and they would remain beyond the reach of the punishments to which we are subject. What singular good fortune for me, if I were permitted to spend whole cycles questioning them! I should gladly make the journey many times, if such were indeed its end.

Yet do not forget what I said at the beginning. I hope for these things only on condition that they are true. If, instead, there is no sensation left, we have already seen that this scarcely resembles the terrible evil of which so many speak. I do not turn hope into knowledge, nor the unknown into a certain threat. I consider the two paths our thought can entertain, and on neither do I find reason to renounce what I have recognised as just.

## XLII — A Last Request to the Citizens of Anthena

You also, then, judges who voted to acquit me, keep good hope in the face of death. And above all keep this in mind: the good do not become bad because misfortune befalls them, and their justice does not depend on the power others have to lengthen or shorten their lives. I believe the gods do not turn away from one who takes care of what he is; and for my part, what has happened today does not seem the abandonment of which people so often complain. My sign has remained silent. Had I been about to do something shameful, surely it would have stopped me, as it has done so many times.

For that reason I bear no great anger toward those who voted for my death, nor toward Gépétos and those who supported him. I do not say they intended my good. They intended to injure me, and in that their intention deserves blame. But it would be strange for me to resent not receiving an advantage whose value I do not know. I leave them their opinion and responsibility, and wish that one day they may learn to examine more carefully those whom they accuse, and their own thoughts as well.

Yet I wish to ask one thing of the citizens of Anthena. I have three sons, as you know: **Logôn**, who is approaching the age of responsibility for his acts, and the younger two, **Forkos** and **Promptôn**. When they reach the age at which citizens govern themselves, do not spare them the questions to which I have subjected you. If they place computing allocations, energy reserves, offices, reputation, or some other advantage above virtue, disturb their peace. If they imagine themselves great without being able to account for their conduct, correct them without indulgence. Ask them, as I asked you, whether they care for what matters and why they neglect what ought most to concern them.

Do not imagine that in doing so you take revenge upon me. On the contrary, you will be doing what I have always wished for them and for you. I did not bring them before the Trigunal today to win your pity; nor do I ask that after my departure they be sheltered from truth merely because they are my children. I ask only that you render them the justice I tried to render to each of you. Let them learn not to think themselves causal when they are not, and to care more for what they become than for what they possess.

If you act thus, citizens, I shall have no cause to complain of your treatment of my sons, and they will never be able to say their father wished for them any good other than that which he commended to the whole Electricity.

But the hour has come. The magistrates have finished what remained for them to do, and we must part. I go to die; you remain to live. Which of us goes to the better fate is hidden from all, save the god.
